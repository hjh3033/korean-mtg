Magic in Korea, 1995–1998 · Part 1

Winter 1996: how I found Magic in Busan

By Jeff Hyun · October 2026 · 8 min read

This series is about one thing: what it was actually like to play Magic: The Gathering in Busan, Korea, in the late 1990s. Not the card list — the collector's guide has that — but the rooms, the people, the prices, and the small accidents that decide whether a fifteen-year-old keeps a hobby or drops it. I am writing it from memory, with the help of the cards themselves, which I still have. Where I am not sure, I will say so.

A reading room, winter 1996

I first saw Magic in the winter of 1996, in a dokseosil — one of the private study rooms Korean students rent by the month to cram in. A group of seniors one year above me used the same room, and during breaks we hung around together in the lounge. The one who studied hardest, the top student of the group, was also the one who brought a stack of Magic cards and played with the others in the lounge. I watched. One evening they wanted to play two-on-two and were a player short, so I was pulled in, and that was that.

A few weeks later I bought their leftovers. Three thousand five hundred won — less than the price of two booster packs at the time — for a pile of a hundred or so surplus cards, a mix of Fourth Edition, Mirage and Visions. I had been told only the most basic rules; everything else I picked up by playing and losing. What hooked me was not winning. It was building a deck of my own and finding out, one game at a time, what it did. I lost whole evenings to it.

Then the seniors became suheomsaeng — final-year students facing the university entrance exam, which in Korea means everything else stops. They quit. The expensive cards they sold off; the rest they passed down to me. I was the only one left in the lounge with a deck, and I started looking for somewhere else to play.

Nownuri

In 1997 the way you found other players in Korea was either someone you knew, or a BBS. Before the web took over, Korean PC communication ran on dial-up services with text menus — Hitel, Chollian, Nownuri. I was on Nownuri, and it turned out a fair number of Magic players were active there too. Through them I learned that there was a place near my home called Busan Interhobby where people actually met to play. One weekend I took my handful of cards and went.

Busan Interhobby, first visit

I remember the first visit vividly, mostly because of how awkward it was. The space was enormous — far bigger than it needed to be. Looking back as an adult, I have no idea how renting that much floor could have paid for itself. Inside, maybe twenty students, my age or two or three years older, were spread out across the room: duels at some tables, and at others people bent over binders, trading. I had brought two close friends I had introduced to the game, and the three of us stood there not knowing what to do. We had come to play, but we knew nobody, and everyone who was dueling looked like they had arrived together, the way we had. Walking up to a stranger's table was harder than it sounds.

The owner was a woman — young, I think, to have started a business like that — and she came over and asked for the entrance fee. Two thousand won. If you did not want to pay it, she said, you could buy one booster pack instead and that counted as your entry. Two thousand won was roughly what a booster cost then. (For scale: if memory serves, a Big Mac in Korea was about the same price in 1997. I am not certain of that.)

I chose the booster. I am fairly sure it was Korean-language Weatherlight. What I know for certain is what came out of it, because it was the first booster I had ever opened: a Thundermare. There was a magazine in the shop with card prices, and by that magazine Thundermare was worth five dollars. In practice nobody in the room wanted one, so the trade never happened. (I would lose that card later — stolen. That is a story for another part.)

What did get traded was a card from the seniors' hand-me-downs: a Korean Force of Nature, the green 8/8 from Fourth Edition. I gave it up for four copies of Holy Armor, a cheap white enchantment. By the magazine it was a terrible trade. I did not know yet that those four cards would win me a neighborhood tournament. That, too, is for a later part.

What this was, seen from now

None of this was unusual for a Korean teenager in 1997 except the game itself. A study room, a BBS, a hobby shop that charged two thousand won at the door — those were the ordinary infrastructure of the time. Magic ran on top of it for about three years, in a few cities, through a few shops, and then the Korean printings stopped and most of it vanished. The cards from that winter — the 3,500-won bundle, the Thundermare, the Holy Armors — are the reason the collection exists, and the reason I can still check my memory against the cardboard.

Terms for readers outside Korea. Dokseosil (독서실): a private study room rented by the month, rows of partitioned desks and a small lounge. Suheomsaeng (수험생): a student in the final year before the national university entrance exam. Nownuri (나우누리): one of the big Korean dial-up PC-communication services of the 1990s, alongside Hitel and Chollian — text-based forums reached by modem, the Korean equivalent of a BBS. Interhobby: a hobby shop chain name; the Busan branch is the one in this story.
